Research agenda · An evolving set of questions

Supporting reasoning as designs evolve

How can interactive tools help designers recognize, revisit, and reason through design decisions?

Design is a wicked problem. Goals, constraints, and decisions take shape as people explore possibilities, and a local choice can change what matters elsewhere. AI creates new opportunities to move efficiently from an initial goal to a finished artifact. My research focuses on the reasoning and learning that happen throughout the process.

This page outlines a conceptual framework and research directions I am exploring. The directions are questions for future work, not announcements of completed projects.

A design decision space

Two questions organize the agenda: Is a designer aware of a decision that needs attention? And does the designer currently have the knowledge, information, or support needed to resolve it? The answers depend on a particular decision and can change as the design develops.

Not aware · Can resolve

Discovering overlooked decisions

Designers may possess relevant abilities without noticing when those abilities become important to the current design.

Theoretical connections: situated invention; design fixation.

Aware · Can resolve

Reasoning through available choices

Recognizing a decision does not eliminate the need to compare alternatives, reconsider past experience, and explain a choice.

Theoretical connections: design rationale / QOC; situated constructive memory.

Not aware · Cannot resolve

Recognizing knowledge needs

Designers may not yet recognize a consequential question or the knowledge required to address it.

Research connection: awareness of design knowledge needs. Learning support may become relevant after awareness.

Aware · Cannot resolve

Making unresolved decisions shareable

A designer can identify what remains open while the relevant expertise or evidence is distributed across people and contexts.

Theoretical connections: symmetry of ignorance; boundary objects.

These papers illuminate different aspects of the space; none proposes or validates this four-quadrant framework as presented here. Professional experience alone does not establish that a person can resolve a particular decision.

Next research directions

Two emerging questions extend this agenda beyond any single design domain or software. Both concern decisions that may become newly relevant as a design evolves, but they draw attention to different sources of reasoning.

Direction A · Past experience and current judgment

Experience-mediated design reasoning

Design goals and constraints change, so decisions made earlier may need to be reconsidered. Past choices can reinforce, challenge, or extend the way a designer understands a current choice. Yet the relevance of earlier reasoning to the next decision may go unnoticed.

How can interactive design tools help designers draw on and reconsider past decisions to support decision-making as designs evolve?

Theory: situated constructive memory and problem–solution co-evolution. Case-based reasoning provides a complementary perspective on comparing past and present situations.

Direction B · Seeing relationships across a whole design

Situated discovery through re-representation

Designers refine parts locally, while a design operates as an interconnected whole. Changes in goals, constraints, or components can alter relationships that are difficult to perceive from the current view. A different representation, such as an architectural section, can make those relationships available for reconsideration.

How can interactive representations help designers discover overlooked relationships and formulate new design problems as their designs evolve?

Theory: situated invention and the dialectics of sketching.

Design theory across the agenda

The four quadrants are an analytical lens, not four immutable kinds of designers. Two broader theories help explain why awareness and the ability to resolve a decision can change throughout design.

Problem–solution co-evolution

Dorst & Cross (2001). The framing of a design problem and the development of possible solutions influence one another. A decision can gain or lose relevance as both evolve.

Situated function–behaviour–structure

Gero & Kannengiesser (2004). Designing involves changing relationships between the environment, what designers interpret, and what they expect. The same design can prompt different questions at different moments.
