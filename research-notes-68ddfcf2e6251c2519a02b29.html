Research agenda · Working draft

Interactive Support for Informed Design Reasoning

Design rarely begins with a fully specified problem, stable goals, or a known solution. Designers develop their understanding of the problem and possible solutions together through interaction with artifacts, constraints, feedback, collaborators, and the world. My research asks how interactive design tools can support this reasoning while designers retain ownership of their goals and judgments.

How can interactive design tools support informed design reasoning?

01 · Framing

Design develops through consequential decisions

Design problems exhibit characteristics of wicked problems. Goals and constraints cannot always be specified before designing begins, and understanding of the problem develops together with possible solutions. A design move can shape downstream decisions and narrow future possibilities. Designers therefore need to move forward under incomplete knowledge while remaining able to inspect, update, and revise their judgments.

I use informed to describe a decision in which relevant grounds genuinely enter the reasoning while the decision is being formed. These grounds can include design goals and criteria, alternatives, consequences, trade-offs, prior knowledge, uncertainty, and evidence produced through testing. Informed reasoning does not require exhaustive information or guarantee a successful outcome.

Interactive tools shape the external conditions for this reasoning. They can change what designers notice, what knowledge becomes available, what alternatives can be compared, what consequences can be anticipated, and what can be learned by making and testing. The designer continues to define the goal and make the judgment.

02 · Research space

Two questions organize the agenda

The first asks what aspect of the design is being reasoned about. The second asks what form of reasoning the tool supports. Keeping these questions separate distinguishes the object of reasoning from the interaction mechanism used to support it.

What is the designer reasoning about?

A working design-content dimension grounded in design literature. Current candidates include function, behavior, structure, requirements, and process. This dimension remains under development.

×

What reasoning does the tool support?

Six recurring forms of support identified across design and HCI literature: reflection, knowledge retrieval, analogical reasoning, comparison, consequence reasoning, and prototyping.

Reflection

Reconsider the current design and bring overlooked issues, criteria, assumptions, or relationships into attention.

Knowledge Retrieval

Bring relevant existing knowledge, evidence, context, or expertise into the current design activity.

Analogical Reasoning

Use relationships to prior cases, precedents, or other domains to understand the current problem or develop a possible solution.

Comparison

Examine alternatives together to understand their differences, trade-offs, and implications.

Consequence Reasoning

Anticipate and inspect what may follow from a design choice before or while committing to it.

Prototyping

Generate new evidence through making, testing, embodied interaction, and feedback from the world.

These categories describe forms of reasoning support, not interface techniques. A tool may support reflection through critique or re-representation, comparison through juxtaposition, consequence reasoning through simulation, or prototyping through fabrication and testing. The same system can support more than one form.

03 · Current domain

Design to fabrication makes reasoning visible

My current work uses design-to-fabrication as a testbed because digital choices acquire material consequences. Geometry, material, assembly, tolerance, machine constraints, and physical behavior become coupled. Relevant expertise may be distributed across designers, fabricators, engineers, domain experts, and users. Prototypes and fabrication also produce evidence that cannot be fully contained in the digital representation.

This setting makes incomplete knowledge, interdependent decisions, and the cost of premature commitment particularly visible. It also creates concrete opportunities to study when a design tool should support reflection, bring in knowledge, preserve alternatives for comparison, help anticipate consequences, or enable learning through prototypes.

04 · Current projects

Three probes into the research space

Reflection

aCADemy

Explores situated reflective prompts during CAD modeling. The project asks how a design tool can bring relevant but overlooked considerations into ongoing reasoning without taking ownership of the decision.

Comparison

CAMeleon

Explores how designers can keep and compare alternative fabrication approaches before narrowing to one workflow. The project examines how alternatives and their trade-offs can remain available long enough to inform judgment.

Knowledge across collaborators · unresolved decisions

FuzzyCAD

Explores how CAD can represent decisions that are not ready to be resolved and allow relevant expertise to enter through later collaboration. The project treats uncertainty as something designers can express and continue reasoning about in the CAD environment.

05 · Future research

Using the space to generate new research

The agenda treats the two dimensions as a generative space. Different design aspects can require different forms of reasoning support. This opens questions about how designers retrieve relevant expertise for a function or fabrication process, how analogies help reframe an artifact's behavior, how alternatives expose trade-offs in structure, how tools make downstream consequences inspectable, and how prototypes produce evidence that updates goals and criteria.

A second direction concerns orchestration. Reasoning support should enter design activity when it is useful without turning the tool into continuous coaching. Future work can investigate how observable design context can trigger reflection, retrieval, comparison, simulation, or testing while preserving fluent work and designer agency.

A third direction extends beyond individual CAD use. Design reasoning is distributed across people, artifacts, representations, and time. Collaborative design, architecture, product design, and assistive technology provide settings for studying how knowledge and unresolved judgments move across expertise and how interactive tools can keep that reasoning available.
