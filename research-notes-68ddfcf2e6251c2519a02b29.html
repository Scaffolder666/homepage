Interactive Support for Informed Design Reasoning

Design rarely begins with a fully specified problem. A brief may provide initial goals and requirements, but these do not completely determine what the problem is or how it should be solved. Designers continue to develop their understanding of the problem as they develop possible solutions. A proposed solution can expose limitations in the original framing, while a newly recognized constraint can redirect the solution. Research on wicked problems and problem–solution co-evolution describes this as a fundamental condition of design: problem understanding and possible solutions develop together through the act of designing.

Yet designers cannot wait for this understanding to become complete before they act. Design progresses through judgments made while goals, constraints, criteria, and possible solutions are still developing. These judgments are consequential because each can change the situation in which subsequent judgments are made. My research asks how designers can form informed judgments under these conditions. I call a design judgment informed when grounds relevant to the current design situation enter the designer's reasoning while that judgment is being formed. Being informed does not require complete information; designers need sufficient grounds to move forward, and those grounds may change as new evidence or understanding emerges. This progression is difficult because the grounds needed to inform a design judgment do not necessarily become available when that judgment is being formed.

Central thesis. To support informed design reasoning, interactive design tools need to make relevant grounds available as design judgments develop.
How can interactive design tools support informed design reasoning?

I first examine the underlying challenge that prevents relevant grounds from entering a developing judgment. I then develop a research space for interactive reasoning support and describe how I investigate this broader problem through Design-to-Fabrication. Three systems progressively explore how interactive tools can bring grounds into ongoing reasoning, maintain alternatives for comparison, and keep unresolved judgments open to later expertise. I conclude by summarizing the contributions and identifying directions for future research.

1.1 · What is holding back informed design reasoning?

Judgments can become specific before their grounds are available

We begin by taking a closer look at the underlying challenge. Interactive design tools allow designers to make choices even while their understanding of those choices continues to develop. A designer can select an alternative, specify a value, modify a form, or accept a configuration before all of the grounds relevant to that judgment have entered the current design context. The ability to act therefore does not imply that the grounds needed to understand the implications of that action are already available.

This mismatch may matter little when a choice is local, familiar, and easily revised. However, it becomes consequential when a judgment changes what can happen next. A choice can constrain subsequent alternatives, establish dependencies among parts of the design, or shape how later aspects of the problem are understood. In these situations, grounds that are absent from the current judgment can affect a sequence of later judgments.

Consider a designer deciding where to place a component in a developing product. The interface allows the position to be specified immediately. Yet whether that position is appropriate may depend on criteria that become visible only after another component is introduced, on knowledge held by another collaborator, or on evidence produced when the design is later evaluated in use. The geometry can therefore become precise before the judgment that produced it is well grounded.

The same mismatch can arise in several ways. A relevant consideration may already exist but remain outside the designer's attention. Relevant knowledge may exist in a document, a prior case, or another person's expertise but remain disconnected from the current design. A relation to another case may provide a way to understand the current problem. Important differences may become visible only when alternatives are considered together, while downstream consequences may require further reasoning before they become apparent. In other situations, the required evidence does not yet exist and must be generated through making, testing, or interaction.

We conclude that supporting design activity requires more than enabling designers to represent and manipulate increasingly specific design states. A precise representation can still express a judgment formed without relevant grounds, while later evidence can require that an apparently settled judgment be reopened. Interactive support for informed reasoning therefore needs to address what grounds become available to the designer and how those grounds can enter a developing judgment.

1.2 · Supporting informed design reasoning

From the bottleneck to a research space

In this research, I investigate how interactive design tools can make relevant grounds available as design judgments develop. I organize this problem around two questions: (1) what aspect of the design is being reasoned about, and (2) what form of reasoning can help the designer become better informed about it? Together, these questions define a research space for interactive reasoning support. I investigate this space through interactive systems that support different ways for relevant grounds to enter design reasoning.

1.2.1 · What aspect of the design is being reasoned about?

The relevance of a ground depends on what the designer is trying to understand. I ground this dimension in the Function–Behaviour–Structure framework, which characterizes a design object in terms of Function, what it is for; Behavior, what it does; and Structure, what it is. I use these three categories as the first dimension of the research space. They allow reasoning support to be situated in relation to the aspect of a developing design that the designer is currently considering.

1.2.2 · What form of reasoning can help inform it?

The second dimension concerns how relevant grounds can enter reasoning. I synthesize six recurring forms of reasoning support from existing traditions in design cognition and interactive design research. These categories do not constitute an exhaustive taxonomy of design cognition. Broader classifications, such as Hay et al.'s synthesis of conceptual-design cognition, include activities such as problem analysis, concept generation, evaluation, and decision making. My categories address a narrower interaction question: what forms of reasoning can an interactive tool make available to help inform a developing design judgment?

The first dimension is adopted from an existing design ontology. The second is a literature-grounded synthesis for the narrower HCI question of what reasoning an interactive tool can make available.

1.2.3 · A research space for interactive reasoning support

Combining these dimensions produces a research space organized by Design Aspect × Reasoning Support. Each combination represents an opportunity for an interactive tool to support a form of reasoning about an aspect of a developing design. The space does not prescribe a design process, and a system may support several combinations. I use it to locate existing approaches, identify underexplored combinations, and generate new interaction techniques for design reasoning.

Reasoning support specifies what reasoning becomes possible; an interaction mechanism specifies how the interface makes it possible. Reflection may be supported through situated questions or critique, comparison through juxtaposition, and consequence reasoning through simulation or preview. Mechanisms such as externalization, re-representation, and preservation can support several forms of reasoning.

Design-to-Fabrication as an empirical domain

I begin investigating this broader research space through Design-to-Fabrication, where the relationship between design judgments and their grounds becomes particularly observable. Digital choices acquire concrete consequences through geometry, material, assembly, tolerance, and physical behavior. Fabrication and testing generate new evidence that can change how an earlier judgment is understood. Relevant knowledge is also distributed among designers, fabricators, engineers, domain experts, and users. Design-to-Fabrication therefore makes three properties of informed reasoning particularly visible: consequences become concrete, new evidence emerges through making, and relevant knowledge is distributed. I use this domain as a starting point for investigating the broader problem of design reasoning.

1.2.4 · Bringing overlooked grounds into reasoning — aCADemy

Our initial approach addresses situations in which potentially relevant grounds are available to the designer but may not enter the current judgment. aCADemy introduces situated reflective questions during CAD modeling that prompt designers to consider fabrication-related issues as their designs develop. The questions respond to the evolving artifact while leaving designers responsible for deciding whether and how each consideration matters. Through this approach, we investigate whether interactive tools can create moments for overlooked grounds to enter ongoing design reasoning.

1.2.5 · Keeping alternatives available for comparison — CAMeleon

Reflective prompts can bring an issue into attention, but awareness alone may not provide enough grounds to form a judgment. In many design situations, understanding depends on considering alternatives in relation to one another. CAMeleon preserves different fabrication approaches so that designers can inspect how they differ in their implications and trade-offs before narrowing around one approach. Through this approach, we investigate how interactive tools can keep alternatives available long enough for comparative reasoning to inform a developing judgment.

1.2.6 · Keeping unresolved judgments open — FuzzyCAD

Comparison can help designers reason among alternatives, but it still assumes that the current designer has sufficient knowledge to resolve the judgment. In collaborative design, relevant expertise may belong to another person, and some evidence may not yet be available. FuzzyCAD allows designers to leave aspects of geometry unresolved, associate constraints and requests for input with those unresolved judgments, and enable later collaborators to contribute within those bounds. Through this approach, we investigate how interactive tools can preserve unresolved judgments so that additional grounds can enter reasoning across collaborators and time.

Orchestrating reasoning support

Making additional reasoning available does not imply that a system should continuously intervene. Designers also rely on fluent, experience-based activity, and unnecessary intervention can disrupt this activity. Dual-process accounts provide a lens for studying this tension between fluent and more deliberate reasoning. This motivates a further question: How can interactive tools preserve fluent design activity while creating opportunities for deliberate reasoning when additional grounds become consequential? I investigate this question through observable changes in the design context without assuming that a system can infer the designer's internal cognitive state.

1.3 · Contributions

Interactive design tools that support informed reasoning as judgments develop

The main contribution of this research is a framework and set of interactive approaches for making relevant grounds available while designers develop judgments under incomplete knowledge. Existing design tools provide powerful means for creating and manipulating representations, but the ability to specify a design does not ensure that the grounds relevant to that specification have entered reasoning. To investigate this higher-level goal, I make the following contributions:

A framing of informed design reasoning

I characterize an informed design judgment in terms of the relevant grounds available while that judgment is being formed. This framing distinguishes informed reasoning from complete information or successful outcomes and provides a basis for studying how interactive systems can support judgment under evolving design understanding.

A research space for interactive reasoning support

I organize reasoning support along two dimensions: the aspect of the design being reasoned about, grounded in Function, Behavior, and Structure; and the form of reasoning being supported, synthesized as Reflection, Knowledge Retrieval, Analogical Reasoning, Comparison, Consequence Reasoning, and Prototyping. This space connects design cognition to opportunities for interactive system design.

Interactive approaches for bringing grounds into developing judgments

Through aCADemy, CAMeleon, and FuzzyCAD, I develop and study approaches that bring overlooked considerations into ongoing reasoning, preserve alternatives for comparison, and keep unresolved judgments open to additional expertise. Together, these systems show different ways that interactive representations can change when and how relevant grounds become available.

Empirical accounts of reasoning support in Design-to-Fabrication

Across these systems, I study how designers engage with reflective prompts, alternatives, uncertainty, and distributed expertise as they develop designs toward fabrication. These studies identify opportunities and limitations for integrating reasoning support into ongoing design activity.

1.4 · Research agenda

Extending interactive reasoning support beyond the current work

My current work establishes initial approaches to reflection, comparison, and distributed reasoning in Design-to-Fabrication, while leaving substantial parts of the research space unexplored. Future work will investigate how analogical reasoning, consequence reasoning, and evidence generated through prototyping can be integrated into interactive design environments; how these forms of support differ across Function, Behavior, and Structure; and how systems can orchestrate reasoning support without disrupting fluent design activity.

FuzzyCAD further opens a broader direction in distributed design reasoning. Relevant grounds may move among collaborators, representations, simulations, prototypes, and physical artifacts as a judgment develops. This raises a broader question: How can interactive design systems support the movement of relevant grounds across people, representations, artifacts, and time? Together, these directions extend the agenda beyond Design-to-Fabrication toward interactive systems that support informed reasoning across design domains.
