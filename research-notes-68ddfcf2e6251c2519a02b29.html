Research agenda · Work in exploration

How might we support design reasoning in CAD?

I study consequential design decisions through their epistemic state: what designers recognize, what they know they do not know, and what relevant knowledge remains unactivated. My current setting is CAD, where decisions become embedded in geometry and fabrication plans. I investigate how interactive tools can surface overlooked decisions, support reasoning through known uncertainties, and elicit knowledge designers already have but have not yet brought into the decision.

01 · Introduction

Introduction

Design is a wicked problem. Goals, constraints, and possible solutions evolve together, so designers cannot fully plan their work in advance. CAD and AI automation are increasingly effective when a designer already knows what needs to be done and has a clear plan for doing it. My research focuses on the less resolved moments: when an important decision has not yet been recognized, when a designer knows that something is unresolved but cannot yet decide, or when relevant knowledge exists but has not been activated in the current situation. I investigate how CAD interactions can invoke cognitive mechanisms that help designers notice, articulate, and reason through these decisions.

02 · Research framework

Epistemic States of Design Decisions

I treat the unit of analysis as a design decision at a particular moment, not a fixed property of a person. The vertical axis asks whether the designer is aware of the decision or knowledge gap. The horizontal axis asks whether the relevant knowledge, evidence, experience, or expertise needed to resolve it is currently available to the designer or team. Together these dimensions describe four epistemic states: unknown unknowns, known unknowns, unknown knowns, and known knowns.

03 · Research opportunities

Three Epistemic States for Design Support

This agenda focuses on the three states in which interaction can change what designers notice, articulate, or reason about. Unknown unknowns call for surfacing an overlooked decision or gap. Known unknowns call for reasoning support, comparison, evidence, expertise, or ways to keep a decision productively open. Unknown knowns call for eliciting or activating knowledge that is already available but not being applied. Known knowns, where the decision and a viable resolution are both clear, are more naturally addressed through execution, acceleration, or automation and are outside my primary research scope.

Cognitive mechanisms are not tied to a single design stage. I treat them as operators on these states: reflection can surface an unknown unknown, comparison or mental simulation can make a known unknown more tractable, externalization and deferred commitment can preserve an unresolved decision, and elicitation or self-explanation can activate an unknown known. The research question is therefore not only which mechanism helps, but when it becomes useful during ongoing CAD work.

Learning, collaboration, and adaptive interaction

Learning to notice. The issue is directing attention to a consequential decision for which the relevant grounds are already available. Geometry-linked critiques or prompts could surface overlooked constraints without deciding for the user.

Collaboration. Another perspective can expose dependencies that an individual did not notice. Such discoveries should remain attached to the relevant model geometry and be available across handoffs.

Adaptation. A suggested interface might reveal decision-relevant context first, then expose comparison or guidance tools if a new gap appears. RQ17 addresses changes between situations, not a permanent label for the designer.

Learning, collaboration, and adaptive interaction

Recognizing missing grounds. A system might surface an overlooked fabrication constraint, missing test result, or incorrect assumption without immediately resolving the design. Embedded design critics are a precedent for contextual feedback. Geometry-anchored critiques and reflective questions are possible interactions, not established learning outcomes. Here both the decision and the missing grounds may be invisible to the designer.

Collaboration. Multidisciplinary review can surface blind spots and reveal which expertise or evidence is not represented in the current conversation.

Adaptation. An interface could first help a designer recognize the issue, then offer learning resources, a test, or expert input as the need becomes explicit. Inferred understanding is uncertain, so users should be able to dismiss, override, or switch the suggested view.

Learning and collaboration: current considerations

Situated learning. Learning on demand motivates explanations inside an ongoing design activity. Examples, expert input, and guidance that fades could help users work through a known gap. A completed decision is not itself proof of independent learning; test subsequent decision recognition, explanations, and performance after help is removed.

Knowledge across people. A shared CAD model can retain unresolved aspects, constraints, rationale, and partial contributions. Research needs to distinguish whether collaborators resolved a decision or only advanced it, and whether handoffs retain the context needed by the next person.

Design-to-fabrication evidence. An open question is whether support during modeling helps designers understand and apply fabrication knowledge. Possible evidence includes recognizing process constraints, explaining manufacturing choices, revising CAD in response to prototype or fabrication feedback, and reasoning independently in a later task. Physical fabrication feasibility should be examined when a prototype is made, but a successful artifact alone does not demonstrate that its designer learned. This is about reasoning from design to fabrication, not scoring AI-generated geometry.

Comparison and reconsideration. Comparing alternatives, making provisional predictions, and revisiting them when evidence changes can develop decision grounds. Parallel exploration is an interaction opportunity, not proof that the grounds are sufficient or that learning occurred.

Adaptive interfaces: shared model, mechanisms and possible study

Research opportunity. Distinct roles and design tasks may call for different interfaces to the same underlying design. The research opportunity is matching interactions to changing reasoning needs, not simply generating alternative layouts.

Shared representation. One model and data structure might hold geometry, constraints, open decisions, alternatives, rationale, evidence, and provenance. A designer could explore alternatives, an engineer inspect loads, and a physical therapist assess movement using different views of the same decision objects. A single person might also change views as the decision evolves.

Adaptation and continuity. Decision awareness and adequacy of grounds suggest possible triggers for surfacing overlooked choices, comparing alternatives when their grounds are available, offering situated learning, or supporting a handoff when evidence or expertise is missing. These states are research hypotheses, not reliable automatic diagnoses. Suggested views should explain the reason for changing, remain user-controlled, and preserve geometry references, selection, history, and unresolved questions.

Research questions. What evidence indicates a change in reasoning need? What should adapt: information, available operations, modality, or amount of guidance? How do people retain context and decision ownership when switching between specialized views? How can different collaborators maintain shared understanding while seeing different interfaces?

Possible study. Prototype interchangeable views backed by one model and decision schema. Compare a fixed interface, manually chosen task or role views, and context-sensitive suggestions during a multi-stage CAD task or handoff. Examine decision recognition, reasoning, information lost in transitions, handoff accuracy, interruption, and perceived control. This is a proposal, not an implemented system or established finding.

04 · Future research

Future Research

Cognitive mechanisms at the right moment

When should CAD invoke mechanisms such as reflection, comparison, mental simulation, reframing, decomposition, externalization, or elicitation? Rather than assigning each mechanism to a fixed design stage, I am interested in how its value depends on the epistemic state of a particular decision and on what is happening in the model at that moment.

Unknown knowns

How might CAD help designers retrieve, articulate, or externalize relevant knowledge they already possess but have not yet connected to the current geometry or decision? This includes tacit fabrication experience, prior examples, disciplinary judgment, and requirements that become relevant only when a particular configuration appears.

Beyond CAD

Which epistemic decision states and cognitive mechanisms transfer to other design practices, and which require domain-specific representations, tools, and expertise?
