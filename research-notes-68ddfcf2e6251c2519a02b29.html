Supporting Informed Design Reasoning in CAD

Design problems have long been characterized as wicked because they cannot be fully specified before designing begins (Rittel & Webber, 1973). Designers develop their understanding of goals, requirements, and constraints while exploring possible solutions (Cross, 2006; Dorst & Cross, 2001). Emerging solutions can in turn reshape how the problem itself is understood (Dorst & Cross, 2001; Lawson, 1979; Suwa et al., 2000).

Designers therefore have to make decisions while both the design and their understanding are still developing. As the design progresses, new considerations can surface and earlier decisions may need to be revisited or reframed (Schön, 1983; Suwa et al., 2000). Design reasoning consequently continues alongside the developing design.

CAD tools mediate many of these decisions by giving designers representations through which they specify, inspect, and modify a design. This makes CAD an important site for supporting the reasoning through which designers decide how the design should develop. My research asks:

How might we support informed design reasoning in CAD as a design develops?

In this chapter, I first examine what makes informed design reasoning difficult to support in current CAD tools. I then introduce a research space that organizes what designers reason about and the forms of reasoning that can support it, and locate my current work within this space. Finally, I summarize the contributions of this research agenda and outline the structure of the thesis.

1.1 · What is holding back informed design reasoning?

We begin by taking a closer look at the underlying challenge. A longstanding trajectory in computer-aided design (CAD) research is to make creation easier and faster (Igarashi, 2025). CAD tools support this trajectory by making it increasingly easy to specify and modify precise geometry through modeling features, parameters, and constraints (Gross & Do, 1996; Camba & Contero, 2015).

However, a precise CAD model does not necessarily reveal what the designer is reasoning about. CAD tools can require designers to specify geometry precisely even while their intentions remain ambiguous or exploratory (Gross & Do, 1996). The model records the resulting geometry, but the same geometric representation can reflect different design concerns. A specific dimension or spatial placement may respond to a functional requirement that the design must accomplish, an evaluation criterion used to judge a design choice, a physical constraint that bounds possible solutions, or a possible solution being explored. These concerns require different information to be developed before a designer can make an informed decision. Yet this distinction is not necessarily visible in the geometry itself. Consequently, design intent and the reasoning behind prior modeling actions can be difficult to interpret from the resulting CAD artifact or workflow alone (Cheng et al., 2026).

Knowing what a decision concerns is also not sufficient to support it. Designers can address the same design concern through different forms of reasoning. To address a constraint, a designer might reflect on the current design, retrieve relevant domain knowledge, draw from an analogous case, compare alternative directions, anticipate consequences, or generate new evidence through prototyping (Schön, 1983; MacLean et al., 1991; Ball & Christensen, 2019). These different forms of reasoning can call for different forms of tool support. Current CAD tools provide extensive support for specifying and modifying geometry, while these forms of reasoning receive less direct support.

These two challenges limit how CAD tools support informed design reasoning. Supporting a decision requires understanding both what aspect of the design the designer is reasoning about and what form of reasoning can help them address it. Without the first, support cannot be directed toward the relevant design concern. Without the second, it is unclear what kind of reasoning the tool should support.

I therefore organize this research agenda around two questions: what are designers reasoning about, and what forms of reasoning help them move the design forward? The following section develops these two dimensions through a synthesis of design literature and uses their intersection to construct a research space for informed design reasoning in CAD.

1.2 · Research Agenda

In this thesis, I investigate how CAD tools can support informed design reasoning through a Design Reasoning Space that intersects four design concerns (Goals / Requirements, Criteria, Constraints, and Possible Solutions) with six forms of reasoning (Reflection, Knowledge Retrieval, Analogical Reasoning, Comparison, Consequence Reasoning, and Prototyping). Each intersection identifies a family of questions about how a particular form of reasoning can help designers address a particular concern as a design develops. My current work investigates three intersections in this space: (1) supporting reflection on design criteria, (2) comparing alternative possible solutions, and (3) retrieving relevant knowledge for developing possible solutions. Each project develops a CAD tool that probes one of these intersections, while the broader space identifies opportunities beyond the work completed so far.

1.2.1 · What are designers reasoning about?

The first dimension concerns what designers need to understand or develop as they move a design forward. I synthesize four recurring concerns from established accounts of design reasoning. These are working labels rather than an existing four-part taxonomy. Prior work distinguishes needs and requirements, criteria for assessing design options, constraints that bound possible designs, and the development of possible solutions. I then pressure-test this synthesis against the interaction precedents in my literature archive and across the reasoning forms in the second dimension.

That test identified Possible Solutions as necessary to account for reasoning about possible realizations that cannot be reduced to goals, criteria, or constraints. The principal unresolved boundary remains Goals / Requirements: requirements can encode goals, criteria, and constraints, so their placement requires further validation.

This decomposition also separates several cross-cutting properties that initially appeared as candidate categories. User needs can provide grounds for goals, criteria, or constraints. Relationships connect concerns and decisions. A concern can remain unresolved, and any concern can be overlooked. I therefore treat source, relationship, resolution state, and awareness state as properties of reasoning situations rather than parallel values of this dimension.

1.2.2 · What forms of reasoning help designers address a design decision?

The second dimension concerns how designers reason about these concerns as the design develops. I synthesize six recurring forms of reasoning from established traditions in design cognition and design research. These categories do not constitute an exhaustive taxonomy of design cognition. Broader classifications, such as Hay et al.'s synthesis of conceptual-design cognition, include activities such as problem analysis, concept generation, evaluation, and decision making. Here I focus on forms of reasoning through which designers can reconsider a situation, bring relevant knowledge into it, draw from analogous cases, compare alternatives, anticipate consequences, or generate new evidence.

1.2.3 · Supporting reflection on design criteria (aCADemy)

Our initial approach examines how reflection can help designers recognize and reconsider criteria relevant to a developing design (Criteria × Reflection). As identified above, CAD tools can make a geometric choice explicit without making the considerations that inform that choice equally visible. This can make it difficult for designers to recognize factors that have not yet entered their reasoning.

We investigate this intersection through aCADemy, which introduces situated reflective questions during CAD modeling. The questions respond to the developing model and prompt designers to consider fabrication-related issues that may otherwise remain overlooked. Designers can defer, reject, or act on these questions as they continue modeling, allowing reflection to remain connected to the design activity rather than requiring an immediate response. In this way, aCADemy investigates how reflection can bring additional criteria into an ongoing design decision while preserving the designer's control over how to respond.

1.2.4 · Comparing alternative possible solutions (CAMeleon)

Recognizing relevant criteria does not by itself reveal how different possible solutions perform against them. Designers may need to examine multiple ways of realizing a design before committing to one direction. We investigate this problem through CAMeleon, which examines Possible Solutions × Comparison in the Design Reasoning Space.

CAMeleon supports designers in exploring and comparing alternative design-to-fabrication approaches. By presenting multiple fabrication workflows and their implications, the tool allows designers to examine differences in how a design could be realized and the trade-offs associated with those alternatives. Comparison makes these differences available for reasoning before the designer commits to a particular workflow. CAMeleon therefore investigates how placing possible solutions in relation can help designers use criteria and trade-offs to make an informed choice.

1.2.5 · Retrieving knowledge for developing possible solutions (FuzzyCAD)

Comparison is useful when relevant alternatives and the knowledge needed to assess them are already available. Other design decisions cannot yet be resolved because the current designer lacks the expertise, evidence, or confidence needed to develop a solution further. In collaborative design, that knowledge may reside with another collaborator and become available only later. We investigate this problem through FuzzyCAD, which examines Possible Solutions × Knowledge Retrieval.

FuzzyCAD allows designers to keep specific aspects of a developing design explicitly unresolved while preserving them within the CAD model for subsequent collaborators to address. Designers can mark uncertainty in placement, orientation, size, or shape, record competing alternatives, and associate constraints or contextual information with the corresponding geometry. A later collaborator can inspect these unresolved decisions, contribute relevant expertise, and further develop the design without requiring the earlier collaborator to prematurely resolve the geometry.

In this way, unresolved geometry serves as a shared representation through which relevant knowledge can enter a developing possible solution across collaborators and time. FuzzyCAD investigates how CAD tools can keep a design decision open long enough for distributed expertise to inform it, while still allowing the broader design to continue developing.

1.3 · Contributions

This research develops a framing of informed design reasoning around how designers continue to develop their understanding while making design decisions, and a design reasoning space that relates design concerns to forms of reasoning. The agenda uses this space to formulate questions for CAD tool research and to identify directions that extend beyond the current projects. aCADemy, CAMeleon, and FuzzyCAD provide initial investigations of selected regions of the space.

1.4 · Structure of the thesis

The remainder of this document first establishes the literature underlying the two dimensions of the design reasoning space. It then uses current projects to examine how questions in this space can be investigated through CAD tools and empirical studies. The final section returns to the broader agenda to identify open regions and relations that can guide future research.

6 · Conclusions and Outlook

The design reasoning space makes future work explicit. My current projects investigate three primary intersections. Other regions ask how analogies can shape possible solutions, how anticipated consequences can reshape goals, how prototypes can reveal criteria or constraints, and how relevant knowledge can inform other design concerns. These questions can motivate new CAD tools without requiring the agenda to exhaust every intersection.

Future work can also examine how reasoning moves across the space as a design develops. Comparing possible solutions may reveal new criteria. Prototyping may expose a constraint that changes a proposal. Distributed expertise may change both what designers know and what part of the design needs attention. A second direction therefore studies transitions among reasoning opportunities, including how information and expertise move across people, representations, artifacts, and time.
