Supporting Informed Design Reasoning in CAD

Design problems have long been characterized as wicked because they cannot be fully specified before designing begins (Rittel & Webber, 1973). Designers develop their understanding of goals, requirements, and constraints while exploring possible solutions (Cross, 2006; Dorst & Cross, 2001). Emerging solutions can in turn reshape how the problem itself is understood (Dorst & Cross, 2001; Lawson, 1979; Suwa et al., 2000).

Designers therefore have to make decisions while both the design and their understanding are still developing. As the design progresses, new considerations can surface and earlier decisions may need to be revisited or reframed (Schön, 1983; Suwa et al., 2000). Design reasoning consequently continues alongside the developing design.

CAD tools mediate many of these decisions by giving designers representations through which they specify, inspect, and modify a design. This makes CAD an important site for supporting the reasoning through which designers decide how the design should develop. My research asks:

How might we support informed design reasoning in CAD as a design develops?

In this chapter, I first examine what makes informed design reasoning difficult to support in current CAD tools. I then introduce a research space that organizes what designers reason about and the forms of reasoning that can support it, and locate my current work within this space. Finally, I summarize the contributions of this research agenda and outline the structure of the thesis.

1.1 · What is holding back informed design reasoning?

We begin by taking a closer look at the underlying challenge. A longstanding trajectory in computer-aided design (CAD) research is to make creation easier and faster (Igarashi, 2025). CAD tools support this trajectory by making it increasingly easy to specify and modify precise geometry through modeling features, parameters, and constraints (Gross & Do, 1996; Camba & Contero, 2015).

However, a precise CAD model does not necessarily reveal what the designer is reasoning about. CAD tools can require designers to specify geometry precisely even while their intentions remain ambiguous or exploratory (Gross & Do, 1996). The model records the resulting geometry, but the same geometric representation can reflect different design concerns. A specific dimension or spatial placement may respond to a functional requirement that the design must accomplish, an evaluation criterion used to judge a design choice, a physical constraint that bounds possible solutions, or a possible solution being explored. These concerns require different information to be developed before a designer can make an informed decision. Yet this distinction is not necessarily visible in the geometry itself. Consequently, design intent and the reasoning behind prior modeling actions can be difficult to interpret from the resulting CAD artifact or workflow alone (Cheng et al., 2026).

Knowing what a decision concerns is also not sufficient to support it. Designers can address the same design concern through different forms of reasoning. To address a constraint, a designer might reflect on the current design, retrieve relevant domain knowledge, draw from an analogous case, compare alternative directions, anticipate consequences, or generate new evidence through prototyping (Schön, 1983; MacLean et al., 1991; Ball & Christensen, 2019). These different forms of reasoning can call for different forms of tool support. Current CAD tools provide extensive support for specifying and modifying geometry, while these forms of reasoning receive less direct support.

These two challenges limit how CAD tools support informed design reasoning. Supporting a decision requires understanding both what aspect of the design the designer is reasoning about and what form of reasoning can help them address it. Without the first, support cannot be directed toward the relevant design concern. Without the second, it is unclear what kind of reasoning the tool should support.

I therefore organize this research agenda around two questions: what are designers reasoning about, and what forms of reasoning help them move the design forward? The following section develops these two dimensions through a synthesis of design literature and uses their intersection to construct a research space for informed design reasoning in CAD.

1.2 · The Design Reasoning Space

In this thesis, I investigate how interactive CAD tools can support informed design reasoning as a design develops. I organize this agenda through a Design Reasoning Space that relates two questions: what are designers reasoning about, and what forms of reasoning can help them do so? The space crosses four design concerns with six forms of reasoning. Each intersection defines a family of research questions about how a particular form of reasoning can support a particular concern during design.

1.2.1 · What are designers reasoning about?

The first dimension describes what aspect of a developing design is under consideration. Drawing on established accounts of design reasoning, I synthesize four recurring concerns: Goals / Requirements, Criteria, Constraints, and Possible Solutions. These categories provide working labels for distinguishing different objects of design reasoning. They do not assume that prior literature uses this exact four-part taxonomy.

I test the coverage of this synthesis against the interaction precedents in my literature archive. This process asks whether each precedent can be described using the four concerns and where the categories fail to account for a case. Possible Solutions emerged as necessary because many interactions support reasoning about how a design might be realized, which cannot be fully described as reasoning about goals, criteria, or constraints.

Dimension 1 is a literature-grounded synthesis, not a claim that prior work proposes this exact four-category taxonomy. The interaction-precedent archive is used as a negative-case and coverage test. The principal unresolved boundary remains Goals / Requirements: requirements can encode goals, criteria, and constraints, so their placement requires further validation.

This decomposition also separates several properties that cut across the four concerns. User needs may inform goals, criteria, or constraints. Relationships can connect multiple concerns or decisions. Any concern may remain unresolved, and any concern may initially go unnoticed. I therefore treat source, relationship, resolution state, and awareness state as properties of a reasoning situation, not as additional values of this dimension.

1.2.2 · What forms of reasoning help designers?

The second dimension describes the reasoning activity through which designers develop or reconsider these concerns. I synthesize six recurring forms of reasoning from research in design cognition and related fields: Reflection, Knowledge Retrieval, Analogical Reasoning, Comparison, Consequence Reasoning, and Prototyping.

Analogical Reasoning draws relationships from prior cases or other domains. Comparison examines differences among alternatives in relation to relevant criteria. 1.3 · Interactive Tools for Design Reasoning

My current work investigates three intersections in the Design Reasoning Space through interactive CAD tools: Criteria × Reflection, Possible Solutions × Comparison, and Possible Solutions × Knowledge Retrieval. Each project examines how a different form of reasoning can be supported within an ongoing CAD workflow.

1.3.1 · Supporting reflection on design criteria

The first project investigates how reflection can help designers reconsider the criteria shaping a developing model (Criteria × Reflection). CAD interfaces make geometric operations explicit, but the criteria behind those operations may remain unexamined as modeling proceeds. A designer may change an angle, dimension, or feature without reconsidering what that choice needs to accomplish.

We investigate how situated reflective questions can bring these criteria into the ongoing modeling process. The tool presents questions in response to design activity and allows designers to engage with them while continuing to work on the model. Designers can respond to a question, defer it, dismiss it, or continue modeling. This interaction gives designers opportunities to articulate and reconsider relevant criteria without requiring the tool to determine the design goal or prescribe a solution.

1.3.2 · Comparing alternative possible solutions

The second project investigates how comparison can support reasoning across alternative ways of realizing a design (Possible Solutions × Comparison). When several approaches remain plausible, designers need to understand how those alternatives differ before deciding which direction to develop further.

We investigate how a CAD environment can keep alternative design-to-fabrication approaches available for structured comparison. The tool presents alternatives within a shared environment so that designers can inspect differences in their geometry, fabrication implications, and relevant trade-offs. Designers can use these comparisons to examine how different realizations respond to the requirements and constraints of the current design while retaining control over which direction to pursue.

1.3.3 · Retrieving knowledge for developing possible solutions

The third project investigates how knowledge retrieval can support possible solutions that cannot yet be fully specified (Possible Solutions × Knowledge Retrieval). In collaborative design, a developing solution may depend on expertise, evidence, or contextual knowledge held by another collaborator. CAD representations, however, typically require designers to encode geometry through specific dimensions and forms. This makes it difficult to express that part of a solution is intentionally unresolved and still requires input.

We investigate how unresolved geometric decisions can remain explicit within the CAD model while carrying the information another collaborator needs to continue reasoning about them. The tool allows designers to mark aspects of geometry as unresolved and associate them with relevant constraints, questions, and context. Later collaborators can inspect these decisions, contribute relevant expertise, and refine the geometry as new information becomes available.

This interaction makes the unresolved decision itself part of the collaborative handoff. A designer can move the broader model forward without committing to a geometric choice that still depends on knowledge from someone else.

1.4 · Contributions

This research develops a framing of informed design reasoning around how designers continue to develop their understanding while making design decisions, and a design reasoning space that relates design concerns to forms of reasoning. The agenda uses this space to formulate questions for CAD tool research and to identify directions that extend beyond the current projects. aCADemy, CAMeleon, and FuzzyCAD provide initial investigations of selected regions of the space.

1.5 · Structure of the thesis

The remainder of this document first establishes the literature underlying the two dimensions of the design reasoning space. It then uses current projects to examine how questions in this space can be investigated through CAD tools and empirical studies. The final section returns to the broader agenda to identify open regions and relations that can guide future research.

6 · Conclusions and Outlook

The design reasoning space makes future work explicit. My current projects investigate three primary intersections. Other regions ask how analogies can shape possible solutions, how anticipated consequences can reshape goals, how prototypes can reveal criteria or constraints, and how relevant knowledge can inform other design concerns. These questions can motivate new CAD tools without requiring the agenda to exhaust every intersection.

Future work can also examine how reasoning moves across the space as a design develops. Comparing possible solutions may reveal new criteria. Prototyping may expose a constraint that changes a proposal. Distributed expertise may change both what designers know and what part of the design needs attention. A second direction therefore studies transitions among reasoning opportunities, including how information and expertise move across people, representations, artifacts, and time.
