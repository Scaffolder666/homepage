Interactive Support for Informed Design Reasoning

Design rarely begins with a fully specified problem. A brief may provide initial goals and requirements, but these do not completely determine what the problem is or how it should be solved. Designers continue to develop their understanding of the problem as they develop possible solutions. A proposed solution can expose limitations in the original framing, while a newly recognized constraint can redirect the solution. Research on wicked problems and problem–solution co-evolution describes this as a fundamental condition of design: problem understanding and possible solutions develop together through the act of designing.

Yet designers cannot wait for this understanding to become complete before they act. Design progresses through judgments made while goals, constraints, criteria, and possible solutions are still developing. These judgments are consequential because each can change the situation in which subsequent judgments are made. My research asks how designers can form informed judgments under these conditions. I call a design judgment informed when grounds relevant to the current design situation enter the designer's reasoning while that judgment is being formed. Being informed does not require complete information; designers need sufficient grounds to move forward, and those grounds may change as new evidence or understanding emerges. This progression is difficult because the grounds needed to inform a design judgment do not necessarily become available when that judgment is being formed.

Central thesis. To support informed design reasoning, interactive design tools need to make relevant grounds available as design judgments develop.
How can interactive design tools support informed design reasoning?

I first examine why relevant grounds can remain unavailable while a design judgment develops. I then define a research space that relates what a judgment needs to address to the forms of reasoning that can help address it. My current work investigates selected regions of this space through interactive systems in Design-to-Fabrication. The remaining regions and the relations among them provide directions for future research.

1.1 · What is holding back informed design reasoning?

Judgments can become specific before their grounds are available

We begin by taking a closer look at the underlying challenge. Interactive design tools allow designers to make choices even while their understanding of those choices continues to develop. A designer can select an alternative, specify a value, modify a form, or accept a configuration before all of the grounds relevant to that judgment have entered the current design context. The ability to act therefore does not imply that the grounds needed to understand the implications of that action are already available.

This mismatch may matter little when a choice is local, familiar, and easily revised. However, it becomes consequential when a judgment changes what can happen next. A choice can constrain subsequent alternatives, establish dependencies among parts of the design, or shape how later aspects of the problem are understood. In these situations, grounds that are absent from the current judgment can affect a sequence of later judgments.

Consider a designer deciding where to place a component in a developing product. The interface allows the position to be specified immediately. Yet whether that position is appropriate may depend on criteria that become visible only after another component is introduced, on knowledge held by another collaborator, or on evidence produced when the design is later evaluated in use. The geometry can therefore become precise before the judgment that produced it is well grounded.

The same mismatch can arise in several ways. A relevant consideration may already exist but remain outside the designer's attention. Relevant knowledge may exist in a document, a prior case, or another person's expertise but remain disconnected from the current design. A relation to another case may provide a way to understand the current problem. Important differences may become visible only when alternatives are considered together, while downstream consequences may require further reasoning before they become apparent. In other situations, the required evidence does not yet exist and must be generated through making, testing, or interaction.

We conclude that supporting design activity requires more than enabling designers to represent and manipulate increasingly specific design states. A precise representation can still express a judgment formed without relevant grounds, while later evidence can require that an apparently settled judgment be reopened. Interactive support for informed reasoning therefore needs to address what grounds become available to the designer and how those grounds can enter a developing judgment.

1.2 · Interactive support for informed design reasoning

From the bottleneck to a research space

To investigate how interactive tools can make relevant grounds available, I first characterize the design reasoning they are intended to support. Grounds become relevant in relation to what a developing judgment needs to establish and how designers reason about it. I therefore organize the agenda along two dimensions: what needs to be addressed in a design judgment, and what forms of reasoning help designers address it. Their intersection defines a design reasoning space that locates opportunities for interactive support.

1.2.1 · What needs to be addressed in a design judgment?

The first dimension concerns what the developing judgment needs to establish. I synthesize four recurring concerns from established accounts of design reasoning. These are working labels rather than an existing four-part taxonomy. Prior work distinguishes needs and requirements, criteria for assessing design options, constraints that bound possible designs, and the development of possible solutions. I then pressure-test this synthesis against the interaction precedents in my literature archive and across the reasoning forms in the second dimension.

Dimension 1 is a literature-grounded synthesis, not a claim that prior work proposes this exact four-category taxonomy. The interaction-precedent archive is used as a negative-case and coverage test. That test identified Design Proposal as necessary to account for reasoning about possible realizations. The principal unresolved boundary remains Goals / Requirements: requirements can encode goals, criteria, and constraints, so their placement requires further validation.

This decomposition also separates several cross-cutting properties that initially appeared as candidate categories. User needs can provide grounds for goals, criteria, or constraints. Relationships connect concerns and decisions. A concern can remain unresolved, and any concern can be overlooked. I therefore treat source, relationship, resolution state, and awareness state as properties of reasoning situations rather than parallel values of this dimension.

1.2.2 · What forms of reasoning help designers address a design judgment?

The second dimension concerns how designers develop the grounds needed to address a judgment. I synthesize six recurring forms of reasoning from established traditions in design cognition and design research. These categories do not constitute an exhaustive taxonomy of design cognition. Broader classifications, such as Hay et al.'s synthesis of conceptual-design cognition, include activities such as problem analysis, concept generation, evaluation, and decision making. Here I focus on forms of reasoning through which designers can reconsider a situation, bring relevant knowledge into it, draw from analogous cases, compare alternatives, anticipate consequences, or generate new evidence.

Both dimensions are literature-grounded working syntheses. The first distinguishes what a developing judgment needs to establish. The second distinguishes forms of reasoning through which designers can develop the grounds needed to address it. The precedent archive provides negative-case and coverage tests for the resulting space.

1.2.3 · From the research space to interactive investigations

Each intersection in this space identifies a family of research questions. For example, the intersection of Criteria and Reflection asks how reflection can help designers recognize or reconsider what matters to a developing judgment. Design Proposal and Comparison asks how comparing alternatives can inform the development of a proposal. Design Proposal and Knowledge Retrieval asks how relevant knowledge or expertise can enter the development of a proposal. I use these intersections to locate research questions, not as a checklist to be exhaustively covered.

A research project begins with a primary question in this space and investigates how an interactive tool can support the corresponding reasoning. The project may also encounter neighboring questions as the judgment develops. I distinguish the reasoning form from the interaction mechanism used to support it. Reflection or comparison describes how a designer reasons. A situated prompt, juxtaposed alternatives, or a provisional representation describes how an interface creates conditions for that reasoning. This separation allows different interaction mechanisms to investigate the same region of the space and allows one mechanism to support reasoning across several regions.

Locating current work in the research space

The research space is broader than the work I have completed so far. My current projects provide initial investigations of three intersections. aCADemy examines Criteria × Reflection, CAMeleon examines Design Proposal × Comparison, and FuzzyCAD examines Design Proposal × Knowledge Retrieval. These locations identify the primary question that motivates each project. As a design judgment develops, each project can also engage neighboring parts of the space.

These projects show how an intersection can guide an interactive systems investigation. They do not set the boundaries of the agenda. The open regions generate further questions. How can prototyping reveal constraints that were unavailable during digital design? How can reasoning about consequences lead designers to reconsider a goal? How can analogical reasoning support the development of a design proposal? The space also supports questions about movement between intersections. Comparing proposals, for example, may reveal that the criteria guiding the judgment need to change.

Design-to-Fabrication as an empirical domain

I begin investigating this broader research space through Design-to-Fabrication, where the relationship between design judgments and their grounds becomes particularly observable. Digital choices acquire concrete consequences through geometry, material, assembly, tolerance, and physical behavior. Fabrication and testing generate new evidence that can change how an earlier judgment is understood. Relevant knowledge is also distributed among designers, fabricators, engineers, domain experts, and users. Design-to-Fabrication therefore makes three properties of informed reasoning particularly visible: consequences become concrete, new evidence emerges through making, and relevant knowledge is distributed. I use this domain as a starting point for investigating the broader problem of design reasoning.

1.3 · Contributions

Contributions

This research develops a framing of informed design reasoning around the availability of relevant grounds and a design reasoning space that relates design concerns to forms of reasoning. The agenda uses this space to formulate questions for interactive systems research and to identify directions that extend beyond the current projects. aCADemy, CAMeleon, and FuzzyCAD provide initial investigations of selected regions of the space.

6 · Conclusions and Outlook

Conclusions and future research

The design reasoning space makes future work explicit. My current projects investigate three primary intersections. Other regions ask how analogies can shape developing proposals, how anticipated consequences can reshape goals, how prototypes can reveal criteria or constraints, and how relevant knowledge can inform concerns beyond a proposal. These questions can motivate new interactive systems without requiring the agenda to exhaust every intersection.

Future work can also examine how reasoning moves across the space as a judgment develops. Comparing proposals may reveal new criteria. Prototyping may expose a constraint that changes a proposal. Distributed expertise may change both the grounds available to a judgment and the concern that needs to be addressed. A second direction therefore studies transitions among reasoning opportunities, including how grounds move across people, representations, artifacts, and time.
